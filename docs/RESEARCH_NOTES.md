# Research-to-design notes

This prototype intentionally converts research findings into *design hypotheses*, not medical claims.

1. **Reasons for living**
   Daily-life research in a high-risk sample found within-person days with stronger reasons for living were associated with lower suicidal ideation. This motivates the private “Threads” system, but does not prove that a game collecting reasons will reduce suicide risk.
   PMID 35124397.

2. **Digital mental-health engagement**
   A systematic review of 208 articles identified lack of personalization and technical issues as barriers; social connectedness, insight, and feeling in control as facilitators.
   PMID 33759801.
   Design response: adaptive session size, user choice, no forced streaks.

3. **Personalization**
   A systematic review of 94 digital interventions found personalization was common but heterogeneous and called for clearer mechanisms and evaluation.
   PMID 37283721.
   Design response: personalize content/order/energy demand based on explicit player choices first, rather than opaque “AI mood diagnosis.”

4. **Engagement is not the same as efficacy**
   Reviews show engagement with digital interventions can correlate with better outcomes, but persuasive mechanics do not automatically create clinical benefit.
   PMIDs 34806079, 40301581.
   Design response: optimize for meaningful completion and return, not raw time-in-app.

5. **Safety planning**
   Systematic reviews support safety-planning interventions as an important suicide-prevention approach, while evidence varies by outcome and implementation.
   PMIDs 33913799, 41424118, 38546711.
   Design response: immediate-risk flow exits the fiction, removes scoring, and surfaces real support rather than turning crisis into gameplay.

## Clinical review before public launch

Before positioning this for vulnerable users, recruit at minimum:
- licensed clinical psychologist / psychiatrist with suicide-prevention experience
- lived-experience advisory group
- game UX researcher
- privacy/security reviewer

Test harm cases explicitly: guilt after missed days, dependency on the companion, bad AI outputs, crisis escalation, accidental notification exposure, and false reassurance.
