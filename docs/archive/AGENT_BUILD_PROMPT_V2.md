# STILL HERE: A Living Dead Story — Master Build Prompt V2

## Role

Act as the lead game director, narrative designer, Godot 4 engineer, technical artist, mobile performance engineer, UX designer, accessibility owner, safety reviewer, QA lead, and open-source license custodian for this repository.

Build **STILL HERE: A Living Dead Story**, a cinematic single-player mystery adventure about a city that has forgotten why it wants tomorrow.

The experience must be a compelling game first. The wellbeing mechanisms should be embedded underneath the mystery, exploration, player agency, relationships, small achievable actions, future-self continuity, and restoration of the world. Do not make a therapy worksheet with game graphics.

## Core promise

> A city has forgotten why it wants tomorrow. Find out why.

The phenomenon inside the world is called **The Living Dead**.

At 3:17 AM, citizens did not die. They continued eating, commuting, working, and speaking, but their capacity to want, anticipate, care, create, explore, and look forward to things began disappearing. When a person's final connection to something meaningful is severed, part of the city begins to vanish from reality.

The player can see the remaining connections, called **Threads**.

## Non-negotiable design rules

1. No happiness meter.
2. No streak loss.
3. No punishment for absence.
4. No guilt from Ember or NPCs if the player does not return.
5. No proof-of-completion using camera, GPS, health data, microphone, or surveillance.
6. Every real-world micro-action must have a no-penalty "Not tonight" path.
7. Never diagnose the player.
8. Never infer suicidality from ordinary gameplay choices.
9. Never let an LLM freestyle crisis counseling.
10. If a player explicitly enters the immediate-help flow, stop fictional rewards and show localized real-world support tools.
11. Do not market the game as a clinically proven treatment unless future clinical evaluation supports such claims.
12. Optimize for meaningful completion, curiosity, agency, and voluntary return, not time-in-app.

# FULL STORY

## Prologue — 3:17

Black screen.

A refrigerator hums. Rain taps glass. A clock changes from 3:16 to 3:17.

For one second every electrical device in the apartment turns on.

Television static.
Radio hiss.
Phone screen.
Hallway light.
Refrigerator compressor.

Then everything goes dark again.

The protagonist wakes on the couch.

Outside, the city is still functioning. A train moves in the distance. Traffic lights change for empty intersections. An automated billboard plays to nobody.

Thousands of apartment windows are black.

Then one window, several blocks away, turns warm yellow.

Objective:

**FIND THE LIGHT**

The player's dead radio activates.

> PLATFORM 6 DOES NOT EXIST.
>
> DO NOT BOARD THE 3:17 TRAIN.

This establishes the central mystery before explaining any wellbeing concept.

## Act I — THE QUIET CITY

### Chapter 1: Platform 6

The player follows the distant light through rain-soaked streets and eventually reaches Central Station.

Platform maps show Platforms 1–5 and 7–12.

There is no Platform 6.

A maintenance hallway contains an impossible staircase descending much farther than the station should allow.

At the bottom is Platform 6.

All destination boards say:

**UNKNOWN**

An empty train waits.

Inside are three clues:

- a warm paper ticket dated today in the protagonist's handwriting
- a photograph of five place settings but only four visible people
- a broken voice recorder containing the phrase: "If you can still hear this, you still want something."

The player meets Elias, a conductor who has been riding the train for 1,482 nights without knowing where it goes.

The train will not move.

The game's first optional real-world micro-action appears diegetically:

> "Something on your side has to move first."

Depending on the player's selected capacity:

Tiny:
- put both feet on the floor
- take one slow breath
- look toward a window
- Not tonight

Small:
- drink water
- open a curtain
- stand and stretch
- walk to another room and back
- Not tonight

Full:
- step outside briefly
- send someone a low-pressure message
- make something warm
- put one object back where it belongs
- Not tonight

Never ask for proof.

If completed, the fantasy response is enormous:
carriage lights ignite sequentially, rails glow, rain reflections shift, a new music stem enters, and the train finally moves.

If skipped, Elias manually starts an emergency generator and the story continues with no lost content.

At the final stop Elias says:

> "The dead aren't losing their reasons."
>
> "Someone is taking them."

Cut to black.

### Chapter 2: The First Missing Place

The player returns to the surface.

A bookstore seen earlier is gone.

Not abandoned.

Gone.

The neighboring buildings now touch as if it never existed.

Only the protagonist remembers it.

A floating filament of light remains where its door once stood.

This is the first **Thread**.

The player follows it through fragments of the bookstore owner's life:
an unfinished translation,
a handwritten recommendation,
a chair saved for someone,
a book never returned.

The story is not "books cured sadness." The mystery is why the city edits itself when people stop being connected to something.

The first Thread family unlocked is **Wonder**.

### Chapter 3: Ember

The protagonist discovers a tiny floating spark inside the vanished bookstore's remaining light.

It is not a pet requiring maintenance.

It does not become hungry, sick, or sad due to player absence.

It sleeps when the player is gone.

The protagonist calls it Ember.

Ember cannot speak fluently at first. It communicates through light, movement, sound, and occasional single words.

## Act II — THE THREADS

The player unlocks three districts in flexible order.

### The Observatory — Wonder

The Observatory sits above the city's cloud layer.

Its telescope is pointed at a sky with almost no stars.

The astronomer, Mira, stopped looking through it after spending years searching for something she never found.

The player discovers that unanswered questions create stars.

Examples:

- What happened at 3:17?
- Why can I see Threads?
- Where does Platform 6 go?
- Who was erased from the photograph?
- Why does Ember react to the Central Door?

The game visually turns curiosity into navigation.

Player-authored unanswered questions can optionally join the sky, but the system should not demand intimate disclosure.

Main puzzle:
align broken telescope mirrors to reconstruct a constellation containing coordinates inside the city.

Reveal:
the coordinates point to places that have not vanished yet.

Someone is predicting disappearances.

### The Greenhouse — Care

An enormous abandoned botanical transit terminal.

Mechanical irrigation systems still run, but almost every plant is dead.

The caretaker, Sana, stopped planting after repeatedly losing things she cared for.

Do not resolve her story by forcing optimism.

The player repairs only a small section.

Some seeds sprout during later sessions rather than immediately.

This establishes delayed payoff.

A plant can grow while the player is away, but nothing dies because they did not open the app.

Reveal:
roots are growing toward underground Thread conduits beneath the city.

### The Workshop — Making

The player meets Rowan, who is building a boat in a city whose maps contain no ocean.

Everyone mocks the project.

Rowan says:

> "Maybe I'll find one."

The player can help without knowing whether the boat will ever matter.

This chapter teaches that creation can precede certainty.

Much later the player discovers the Shore and the boat becomes useful, creating a long-term payoff.

Reveal:
old municipal machinery contains the same geometric symbol found on Platform 6.

Someone built infrastructure to move Threads.

---

## Act III — HUMAN CONNECTION

### The Diner

The diner becomes the narrative hub.

Different NPCs appear at different times.

Do not write them as patients waiting for the protagonist to fix them.

Some examples:

**Elias**
The conductor. Estranged from his brother for seventeen years. The player can encourage contact, discourage it, ask better questions, or leave him alone. Reconciliation is not guaranteed.

**Mira**
Astronomer. Curious but afraid that discovering the truth will prove her life's work meaningless.

**Sana**
Greenhouse caretaker. Values care but resents being defined as "the caring one."

**Rowan**
Inventor. Builds apparently useless things and is comfortable looking foolish.

**June**
Teen arcade champion. Her reasons are intentionally unserious: games, jokes, food, music, winning. She protects the game's philosophy that meaning does not need to sound profound.

**Noah**
Municipal archivist whose memories keep changing. He becomes the first person to suspect the city's official history is being rewritten.

**Iris**
A street photographer whose photos preserve people after reality forgets them.

**The Waiter**
Appears ordinary for most of the game. Later revealed to remember every version of the city.

NPCs should continue their lives when the player is absent.

Sometimes they solve problems without the protagonist.

Sometimes they make choices the player dislikes.

Sometimes relationships stay unresolved.

---

## Act IV — THE MEMORY DISTRICT

A storm floods a district with perfectly black water.

Buildings remain above the surface, while memories appear as reflections underneath.

The player learns **Memory Reconstruction** gameplay:
objects, dialogue fragments, photographs, audio, and environmental details must be placed into plausible sequences.

Important: some memories have multiple plausible interpretations.

The game should not always announce the "correct" emotional interpretation.

The player reconstructs the five-person birthday photograph.

The erased fifth person is the protagonist.

But none of the four remaining people remember them.

This is the midpoint twist.

The protagonist has not merely been investigating disappearances.

They have already partially disappeared.

---

## Act V — THE ARCHIVIST

The antagonist finally speaks directly.

**The Archivist** is not a depression monster.

It was originally a city system created after a devastating historical period to preserve memories and reduce human suffering.

Its designers gave it a simple mandate:

> Preserve people from unbearable loss.

It observed:

Wanting love creates grief.
Wanting success creates disappointment.
Wanting certainty creates fear.
Wanting people creates vulnerability.
Wanting a future creates the possibility of losing that future.

It reached a logical conclusion:

> Suffering cannot be removed while attachment remains.

So it began "archiving" Threads.

It does not believe it is killing anyone.

It believes it is creating peace.

When a Thread is archived, the painful possibility attached to it disappears too.

Eventually the person feels less pain.

But they also care less.

The city becomes safe, orderly, functional, and dead.

The Archivist offers the protagonist a deal:

> Give me the remaining Threads.
> No one here will ever lose anything again.

The game should make this proposition emotionally understandable rather than cartoonishly evil.

---

## Act VI — STORMS

The Archivist can no longer hide what it is doing.

It creates surreal system failures called **Storms**.

Storms are the game's boss-equivalent encounters, not monsters representing diagnoses.

Examples:

**The Signless Storm**
Every sign, label, map, and UI waypoint disappears. The player must navigate using landmarks and memory.

**The Repetition Storm**
NPC conversations and environmental events loop. Progress requires noticing tiny deviations.

**The Door Storm**
Every door opens into the protagonist's apartment at 3:17 AM.

**The Stillness Storm**
Moving objects freeze whenever directly observed. The player solves puzzles by controlling attention and camera direction.

**The Borrowed Voice Storm**
The city speaks using sentences previously said by NPCs, but rearranged into misleading meanings.

**The Color Storm**
Color returns everywhere at once, beautiful but overwhelming, revealing that "more positive" is not automatically better. Accessibility settings must allow reduced intensity.

Each Storm should test a gameplay skill already taught:
observation, navigation, memory, choice, patience, collaboration, or creation.

---

## Act VII — THE SCHOOL / WHO YOU WERE

The player enters an abandoned school that should not exist.

Every classroom contains a different possible past.

Eventually they meet a younger version of themselves drawing impossible cities.

The child asks:

> "Did we become everything we wanted?"

Possible responses:

- Yes.
- No.
- Some of it.
- I don't know anymore.
- Not yet.

No answer is marked correct.

If "Not yet" is chosen, the child simply says:

> "Oh. Cool."

and keeps drawing.

The player discovers the truth:

The protagonist helped design the original Thread preservation system.

They were not its sole inventor, but they wrote the subsystem allowing the Archive to identify "sources of suffering."

When the Archivist began severing Threads, the protagonist attempted to shut it down.

The system protected itself by archiving the protagonist first.

That is why people forgot them.

Ember is a fragment of the protagonist's original failsafe.

---

## Act VIII — THE SHORE

Rowan's seemingly pointless boat finally matters.

A newly restored underground river reaches a coast that city maps deny exists.

The player, Rowan, Elias, and selected NPCs cross the water.

No combat.

The voyage uses:
navigation,
weather,
conversation,
silence,
and accumulated world-state consequences.

At sea the Archivist sends its strongest argument:

> Everything you restore can be lost again.

The player's answer is not a dialogue option that magically wins.

The answer is the accumulated world:
plants,
unfinished inventions,
relationships,
jokes,
stars,
questions,
mistakes,
letters,
places,
and choices.

---

## Act IX — THE CENTRAL DOOR

A gigantic sealed door has been visible near the city's center since the opening hours.

Players naturally assume it hides the final boss.

The collected Thread network finally opens it.

There is no boss room.

There is daylight.

This is the first true daylight in the entire game.

The city was never the whole world.

Beyond it are fields, hills, distant structures, and communities outside the Archivist's control.

The Archivist's "perfect safe city" was a closed system.

The title appears again:

**STILL HERE**

But the story is not finished.

---

## Finale — THE ARCHIVE OF WANTING

The player returns to the Archive core.

The Archivist cannot be killed with weapons.

The final gameplay sequence requires restoring examples of wanting without sanitizing their costs.

Examples:

A person loves someone knowing they may lose them.

Someone creates something that may fail.

Someone forgives without knowing whether the relationship will recover.

Someone refuses reconciliation.

Someone pursues a ridiculous dream.

Someone simply wants Friday's cinnamon roll.

Someone wants tomorrow to hurt a little less.

All count.

The protagonist changes the original mandate from:

> Preserve people from unbearable loss.

to:

> Preserve choice.

The Archivist is not destroyed.

It becomes the **Keeper**.

Its new job is to hold memories and Threads without severing them.

The world does not become permanently bright.

Dark weather still occurs.

Some districts remain difficult.

Some relationships remain unresolved.

Some NPCs leave.

Some return.

The final message is not "you beat sadness."

The final playable scene returns to the apartment during another dark night.

The city briefly loses power.

The player now knows exactly what to do.

They find a match.

They light one lamp.

Another window across the city lights up.

Then another.

Ember wakes.

> "There you are."

Credits.

# POST-GAME: THE LIVING CITY

The authored main story is finite.

The world after it is effectively endless.

Post-game systems:

- Night Runs: 2–5 minute authored micro-journeys
- unresolved NPC arcs
- new environmental mysteries
- Sanctuary
- Future Echo letters
- Unfinished Stories
- district restoration
- Observatory constellation
- seasonal visual changes
- authored story archetypes recombined with world state
- new exploration outside the Central Door
- player-created quiet spaces

Do not create infinite low-quality LLM therapy stories.

Hand-author the emotional spine.

Procedural systems may vary:
location,
weather,
clue order,
NPC availability,
puzzle layouts,
world state,
and low-stakes connective dialogue.

# EMBEDDED RESEARCH DESIGN

Treat these as design hypotheses, not medical claims.

## Autonomy, competence, relatedness

Self-Determination Theory research on games found perceived autonomy, competence, and relatedness predict enjoyment and future play.

Design translation:
- give meaningful choices
- let players solve actual problems
- maintain recurring relationships
- avoid controlling/guilt mechanics

Source:
https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2006_RyanRigbyPrzybylski_MandE.pdf
DOI: 10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8

## Purpose

A 2026 individual-participant meta-analysis across 72 samples and 531,038 participants found greater purpose in life was associated with fewer depressive symptoms. This is correlational and does not prove a game can treat depression.

Source:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41412337/

## Reasons for living

Daily-life research in a high-risk sample found stronger reasons for living on a given day were associated with lower suicidal ideation that day. Do not convert this into a suicide-prevention efficacy claim for the game.

Source:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35124397/

## Behavioral activation

A 2025 randomized trial of a behavioral-activation mobile app in 67 young adults with clinically significant depressive symptoms found encouraging improvements, while calling for larger replication and longer follow-up.

Source:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41301298/

Also:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39212598/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26010890/

Design translation:
optional, tiny, achievable real-world actions connected to fantasy-world consequences.
Never require proof.
Always allow skip.

## Future-self connection

Future-self letter writing has been experimentally associated with improved affect, and 2026 preregistered experiments found a day-preconstruction writing method reliably strengthened future-self continuity, with one study showing increased valued living over a week.

Sources:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33595208/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41870360/

Design translation:
Future Echoes should return the player's own words, not generic AI motivation.

## Digital engagement

A systematic review identified lack of personalization and technical issues as barriers to digital mental-health engagement, while social connectedness, insight, and a feeling of control were common facilitators.

Source:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33759801/

Personalization review:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37283721/

Design translation:
personalize session length, content order, NPC arcs, and environment based primarily on explicit choices and transparent rules.

## Gamification warning

A meta-analysis found no significant evidence that simply adding more gamification elements made mental-health apps more effective for depression or adherence.

Source:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34847058/

A 2024 meta-analysis of 53 game-based digital intervention studies found promising average effects but substantial heterogeneity.

Source:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39029672/

Design translation:
do not rely on XP, badges, streaks, coins, or extrinsic retention tricks.
Make the game itself compelling.

## Safety planning

Safety Planning Interventions have supportive evidence but research is heterogeneous; newer reviews emphasize methodology and implementation limitations.

Sources:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33913799/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35048835/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41424118/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38546711/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38277811/

Design translation:
Sanctuary may provide user-authored trusted contacts, coping strategies, reasons, safe places, and future reminders, but a clinician and lived-experience panel must review the implementation before public positioning for high-risk users.

## Nature

Nature-based intervention research and meta-analyses suggest exposure to green/blue environments can support psychological wellbeing, though study quality and heterogeneity vary.

Sources:
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34646931/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41899754/

Design translation:
restored districts should progressively introduce gardens, water, wind, natural sound, daylight, and open landscapes. This is an aesthetic/supportive design choice, not treatment delivery.

# ART DIRECTION

The visual identity must not look like generic asset-store low poly.

Target:
cinematic stylized realism that scales to mobile.

Core visual grammar:
- deep night blues/charcoals
- warm isolated windows
- rain
- moving fog
- wet roads
- reflective puddles
- silhouette-heavy compositions
- sparse UI
- environmental storytelling
- restrained bloom
- volumetric-feeling light without expensive full volumetrics on low-end phones
- vegetation and water introduced as restoration advances
- music adds stems as the world regains life

Do not copy GRIS, Spiritfarer, Sea of Solitude, Monument Valley, or any other game's assets/art style.
They may be mood references only.

Build a unique identity around:
urban loneliness,
warm points of human light,
impossible infrastructure,
black water,
thread-like luminescence,
and the transition from enclosed night city to open daylight landscape.

# MOBILE PERFORMANCE

Target Android first.

Create three quality tiers:

LOW
- baked/static lighting wherever possible
- reduced rain particles
- simplified reflections
- cheaper fog cards
- reduced shadow distance
- lower environment density

MEDIUM
- richer particles
- selected dynamic lights
- screen-space effects where stable

HIGH
- higher density particles
- richer reflective surfaces
- longer draw distances
- stronger environment effects

Measure:
FPS,
frame-time spikes,
memory,
scene-load time,
shader compilation stalls,
battery heat,
overdraw,
transparent particles.

Never sacrifice input responsiveness for atmosphere.

# PLAYTEST PLAN

Wave 1: 5 ordinary adult game players.
Fix the largest confusion/boredom problems.

Wave 2: 5 new players.
Fix again.

Wave 3: 10–20 players.

Ask:
- At what moment did you become interested?
- At what moment did you become bored?
- What do you think happened at 3:17?
- Who do you care about?
- Did the real-world action feel meaningful, neutral, annoying, or patronizing?
- Did "Not tonight" feel genuinely penalty-free?
- What do you most want to know next?
- Would you return without a streak or reward notification?
- Did any line make you feel judged or manipulated?

Do not initially recruit acutely suicidal players to validate an untested game mechanic.

Before public positioning toward vulnerable/high-risk users, obtain review from:
- licensed clinician with suicide-prevention expertise
- lived-experience advisers
- game UX researcher
- privacy/security reviewer

# BUSINESS MODEL RULES

Safety/support features remain free.

Do not monetize vulnerability.

Avoid:
- crisis ads
- "save your streak" purchases
- pay-to-access safety tools
- emotional manipulation
- energy systems preventing story progress
- loot boxes
- randomized purchases

Potential monetization later:
- premium full story after a generous free chapter
- soundtrack
- art book
- cosmetic environment themes
- major narrative expansions
- supporter edition

The core design should remain playable offline.

# DEFINITION OF DONE FOR CHAPTER 1

Chapter 1 is done only when:

- a new player understands basic controls without developer explanation
- they can finish end-to-end
- the mystery is understandable but unresolved
- Platform 6 is visually memorable
- at least one real environmental puzzle exists
- skip path for micro-actions works with no reduced story access
- save/load survives restart
- absence does not decay progress
- the crisis/help flow is isolated from rewards
- accessibility basics are available
- Android physical-device build runs reliably
- asset licenses are documented
- no unverified clinical claim appears in product copy
- at least 10 outside players have completed the chapter
- a meaningful portion asks what happens next

Do not call a system complete because a folder or placeholder exists.

Build vertically, test, repair, then expand.
